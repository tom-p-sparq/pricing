Specifying Conversion Model Parameters

So far, we've introduced conversion modelling, and explored a few example parametric forms that conversion models might take. Having a zoo of potential models is a great start, but to make them useful, we need to make them concrete by setting their parameters. This process turns an abstract model family (like "logistic") into a specific curve that reflects a specific market.

In this post, we'll go deeper into how we specify those models by selecting their parameters. We'll explore two practical methods: defining a model based on its local behaviour at a single price, and defining it by making it pass through two known points.

Local definition

In our conversion modelling zoo we compared all of our species against one another in a single plot. For a fair comparison we gave them all the same behaviour at one specific reference price.

The key observation here is that once a conversion model form (e.g. logistic) has been chosen, it's not enough to specify the conversion rate at a particular reference price. There are many logistic conversion curves that give that conversion probability at that price. If, however, we also specify the local behaviour of the conversion curve around that price, then we uniquely specify the shape of the curve across the entire range of prices.

What do we mean by "local behaviour" in this context? In addition to specifying the conversion probability itself, we can also specify the slope of the curve relating conversion probability to price at this point, via a quantity known as the "elasticity" of the curve. Parametric modellers often refer to the "degrees of freedom" in their model. In our case, all of the models we specified in our zoo have two degrees of freedom. So, specifying both a location (via a price/conversion pair) and a local behaviour (via elasticity at that point) fixes those two degrees of freedom, and is sufficient to uniquely determine the entire curve.

On Elasticity

We have mentioned the word "elasticity" in this post and previously. It is a description of the conversion probability curve in terms of how fast conversion is changing with price. We could use the more classical (and accessible) derivative, $\mathrm d\phi(p) / \mathrm dp$, for this description. However, one problem with this quantity is that it is dimensional: it tells us the percentage point change in conversion rate per unit change in currency. However, a £1 price increase is a very different concept at $p=1$ versus $p=100$. Similarly, a 5% percentage point change in conversion rate is a very different concept at $\phi=0.05$ (where it's a 100% relative change) compared to $\phi=0.5$ (where it's only a 10% relative change).

To address this, economists use a concept called price elasticity of conversion. Instead of looking at absolute changes, elasticity measures the percentage change in conversion for a one percent change in price. This makes it a standardized, "apples-to-apples" measure of sensitivity that can be compared across different prices. For the mathematically curious, it is defined as: $$\epsilon(p) = \frac{\mathrm d\log(\phi(p))}{\mathrm d\log(p)} = \frac{p}{\phi(p)} \frac{\mathrm d\phi(p)}{\mathrm dp}.$$ This formula standardizes the raw derivative by the price and conversion level, making it unitless.

Let's make this concrete with an example. Suppose at a price of $p=£100$, the conversion probability is $\phi(100) = 0.5$ (or 50%). If the elasticity at this price is $\epsilon(100) = -2$, it means that for a 1% increase in price, we expect a 2% decrease in the conversion probability. So, if we increase the price by 1% to $p=£101$, our model predicts the new conversion probability will be approximately $0.5 \times (1 - 0.02) = 0.49$ (or 49%). This gives us a much more intuitive way to think about how sensitive customers are to price changes.

The key consequence of normalisation is that a given value of elasticity has the same meaning across the entire range of prices and conversion probabilities. In other words, elasticity is an economically meaningful value, and we don't need to know its contextual price and corresponding conversion probability to interpret it.

A crucial point to bear in mind is that, while we can specify a model from the elasticity at one point, the elasticity of most models is not constant. As you move along the curve, the elasticity changes. For example, in the logistic model, conversion is typically most elastic around the 50% conversion mark and less elastic at the extremes. This variation is what gives each model its unique character, and makes the choice of model family so important for extrapolating behaviour across a wide range of prices. The "Constant Elasticity" model we saw previously is the exception that proves the rule: its defining feature is that this value does not change.

An alternative specification: interpolation

We saw above that having an estimate of conversion probability at one reference price point is not sufficient to determine the conversion probability curve. This is because the curves we're dealing with have two degrees of freedom: one point only specifies one of those degrees of freedom.

Above, we used elasticity to nail down the other degree of freedom. Alternatively, we can uniquely determine a conversion curve by specifying another point it has to travel through. If we have estimates of the conversion rate at two different prices, then that fixes our two degrees of freedom. So, that's enough to specify the global behaviour of each conversion probability curve across the entire price range.

From playing around with the two methods for specifying the models in our zoo, it becomes clear that the differences between models can only be made very small in small regions of the price range. As we extrapolate further from our reference price(s), and as our interpolants move far apart in price space, our models demonstrate very significant differences in behaviour.

This brings us to a critical question: what do we do when we have conversion data from more than two prices? This is the common scenario when we analyze historical sales data. Unless we are incredibly lucky, it's impossible to draw a two-parameter curve that passes perfectly through three or more data points. Our specification methods (local definition and interpolation) are no longer sufficient.

To solve this, we need to move from exact specification to model fitting. In the next post, we will explore how to fit our models to data, finding the parameters that create a curve that best represents all of our observations, even if it doesn't pass exactly through any of them.
