Fitting Conversion Model Parameters

In the previous post, we introduced the idea of specifying a conversion model by selecting the model parameters. More typically, we are faced with the task of fitting a conversion model to observations of conversions and non-conversions. In this post we'll be able to create synthetic data and see how our model fit responds.

Model fitting

Below, we show the best fit of a logistic model to the data generated above.

We previously talked about having two degrees of freedom in our models. If all of our generated data is at a single price-point, we don't have enough data to choose a model: in the plot above, I have imposed an estimated elasticity of -2 when we only have a single price point. This is important in real world cases: with no price exploration, we cannot fit a model.

As soon as we have produced data at two price points, the curve we fit is now the interpolation between the observed conversion rates. This is clearly the best fit we have to our data.

The moment we generate data at three or more price points, we are in the realm of statistics and data science.

Maximum likelihood estimation

We are looking for the price sensitivity model parameters that best explain our data. To fit these parameters, we quantify the likelihood of the data under those parameters.

If, at any one price point, we are modelling a conversion probability of $\phi(p)$, then (assuming independence of the looks) the likelihood of $k$ conversions from $n$ looks is the binomial probability proportional to $$\phi(p)^k (1 - \phi(p))^{n - k}.$$ For each of multiple price points, $p_i$, then the overall likelihood of the data is proportional to $$\prod_{i=1}^n \phi(p_i)^{k_i} (1 - \phi(p_i))^{n_i - k_i},$$ where we observe $k_i$ conversions from $n_i$ looks at each price point, $p_i$.

The key to fitting our parameters is to observe that this likelihood is a function of the model parameters. We want to choose the parameters going into the function $\phi(p)$ such that this likelihood is maximised. For practical purposes, we maximise the logarithm of this likelihood, $$\sum_i k_i \log\left(\phi(p_i)\right) + \sum_i (n_i - k_i) \log\left(1 - \phi(p_i)\right),$$ which is equivalent.

The animation above that runs at each new datapoint depicts an optimisation algorithm taking the model parameter values to a new optimum that maximises the (log) likelihood of the generated dataset.

Local fitting and new data

The power of this tool is in experimenting: here is one scenario to get started with.

First, let's create a baseline curve based on a moderately significant number of looks and books at four different, but similar prices.

Now observe what happens if we add just one "Convert" click at a higher price, say £180.

Notice how the entire curve shifts to account for this single new data point. The best-fitting model dramatically shifts its opinions not just about prices near £180, but actually also for prices below £140. Why was there any need to lower the estimate of conversion rate at £120 simply because we observed a conversion at £180?

The answer to this is that the model we're trying to fit, having only two degrees of freedom, is in fact severely constrained. This is a crucial lesson: in a parametric model like this, every data point informs the entire curve. Even this single conversion at £120, which had a non-trivial probability (around 1/3) of occurring, was enough to dramatically shift our best-fitting model. Indeed, had that conversion been even less expected (say at £200) then the curve would completely reverse direction, implying conversions being more likely at higher prices.

Conclusion

Fitting a model is not a purely mechanical exercise. The distribution of our data points matters immensely. As we have seen, a single outlier or a narrow band of prices can have a dramatic and sometimes counter-intuitive impact on the model's parameters and its predictions.

A model without a quantification of uncertainty is a key risk: when your data doesn't cover a wide range of prices, your model's extrapolations can be unreliable. The model has no data to learn the "true" elasticity and is just fitting to the local noise. New local data can hugely change our predictions at the extremes of the price range.

In future posts we'll look at how to select between model families, how to quantify uncertainty in a useful, decision-relevant way, and how to think more flexibly about data-driven conversion curves.
