Fitting Conversion Model Parameters

In the previous post, we introduced the idea of specifying a conversion model by selecting the model parameters. More typically, we are faced with the task of fitting a conversion model to observations of conversions and non-conversions. In this post we'll be able to create synthetic data and see how our model fit responds.

Model fitting

Below, we show the best fit of a logistic model to the data generated above.

We previously talked about having two degrees of freedom in our models. If all of our generated data is at a single price-point, we don't have enough data to choose a model: in the plot above, I have imposed an estimated elasticity of -2 when we only have a single price point. This is important in real world cases: with no price exploration, we cannot fit a model.

As soon as we have produced data at two price points, the curve we fit is now the interpolation between the observed conversion rates. This is clearly the best fit we have to our data.

The moment we generate data at three or more price points, we are in the realm of statistics and data science.

Maximum likelihood estimation

We are looking for the price sensitivity model parameters that best explain our data. To fit these parameters, we quantify the likelihood of the data under those parameters. If, at any one price point, we have a conversion probability of $\phi(p)$, then the likelihood of $k$ conversions from $n$ looks is the binomial probability proportional to $$\phi(p)^k (1 - \phi(p))^{n - k}.$$ For each of multiple price points, $p_i$, then the overall likelihood of the data is proportional to $$\prod_{i=1}^n \phi(p_i)^k_i (1 - \phi(p_i))^{n_i - k_i},$$ where we observe $k_i$ conversions from $n_i$ looks at price point $p_i$.

The key to fitting our parameters is to observe that this likelihood is a function of the model parameters. We want to choose the parameters going into the function $\phi(p)$ such that this likelihood is maximised. For practical purposes, we maximise the logarithm of this likelihood, which is equivalent.

The animation above that runs at each new datapoint depicts an optimisation algorithm taking the model parameter values to a new optimum that maximises the (log) likelihood of the generated dataset.

Investigation

We can try out all sorts of different datasets and see what happens. For example, what about when we have few observations at many different prices? When all of the observed prices are in a narrow band, what does that mean for extrapolation beyond that band? Why should fitting a newly observed, low-probability conversion at a high price lower the predicted conversion rate at a lower price?

There's lots to play around with.
