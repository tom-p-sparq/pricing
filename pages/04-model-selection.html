Model Selection from Data

In the previous post, we showed a conversion model being fit to data. We picked one species from our zoo, the logistic model, and implemented the search for the two parameter values giving the best fit to data, by choosing their values to maximise the likelihood of the data. The interactivity in our post showed how the best-fitting model updates as synthetic data on looks and books at different price points accumulate.

In this post, we'll revisit that approach but from the perspective of model selection. We chose logistic previously: why not the log-logistic model, or the Weibulll model? We will think about how you might use the models' fit to data to choose between different possible model families.

Clearly we now have three curves which all demonstrate similar behaviour in the region of the synthetic data. How can we choose between them? And to what extent does our choice actually matter?

Model selection by likelihood

Recall that we are looking for the price sensitivity model parameters that best explain our data. To fit these parameters, we quantify the likelihood of the data under those parameters. For each of our fitted models, therefore, we can keep track of the likelihood, or log-likelihood, of the best fit.

As each our models evolve towards its optimal fit, we display the log-likelihood of each in the figure legend above. The model that demonstrates the maximum value for the log-likelihood is the best fit to the data (noting that log-likelihood is negative, so we aim for the value closest to zero). Assuming no prior preference towards one model or another, this model then becomes the winner.

But how convincing is the evidence that produces this result? Do we believe that one model is actually a better fit than the other?

Likelihood ratios

For any two models, we can directly compare the log likelihoods. We know that all of the models we are considering have just two parameters, so we only need consider the quality of fit. There exist rules of thumb by which we can conclude that the data provide sufficient evidence that any one model is better than the other. For example, we would like a difference in log-likelihoods of at least 1 to even begin to conclude that there is evidence to choose one model over another. For the evidence to count as convincing might require a difference more like 5.

As can be seen from this visualisation of the pairwise differences between our fitted models, it is quite a task to engineer situations where the data allows us to convincingly distinguish between our models. It seems to be a reasonable conclusion that we will find it very difficult to choose one model over another in very many cases.

So does it matter?

The conclusion we're nearing is that we could probably choose any one of our three models, and consider ourselves fairly accurate when it comes to matching the data. At least, until we have hundreds or even thousands of looks at various prices.

But we shouldn't be complacent, because the choice of model does still matter. Recall the model for expected revenue from our introductory post, where the conversion probability multiplies the incremental margin of revenue less cost. Conclusions

The choice of model is, of course, likely to impact the key pricing decision. This is especially true if we need to extrapolate our predictions beyond the historic support of our data. But it is difficult to choose between models for the sparse data we are likely to have. So, where do we go from here?
