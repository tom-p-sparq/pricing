Uncertainty and conversion modelling

Up to now, we've been trying answer the question, "what is the conversion curve?" That is, how does the underlying conversion rate depend on the price? Or, expressed another way, what proportion of the population is willing to pay at least this price? And we've been going about it by trying to find the conversion rate at a given price.

But all we have to go on are a few observations of looks, some of which turn into books. Say we see five looks at a given price and one of those booked. To what extent can we meaningfully conclude that the population-level conversion rate is 20%? Why not a lucky 5%, or an unlucky 50%? It's only five looks from your population, we could easily see a single book by random chance. How unlikely actually is an underlying 50% conversion rate?

That implies that there remains quite a range of possible conversion rates that underlie our observations. So, we need a principled way of accounting for uncertainty in our modelling, which a Bayesian framework gives us very naturally.

Specifying model distributions

Let's focus on the logistic model for now. We've previously talked about how to specify such a model, including specifying its local behaviour at a single reference price, or alternatively specifying its conversion rates at two reference prices. Taking the latter, let's emphasise that we're supplying only estimates of the conversion rate at each of the two reference prices. Together with these specifications, we also supply measures of confidence in them. By changing the specified confidence in the two reference conversion rates, we can push the distribution bands narrower or wider.

Observations

The first useful observation is the interaction between the strength of the prior and the new data. If we set a prior which is very confident in the two estimated conversion rates, say around a sample size of 50, then we need a lot of data to override that. Adding only a handful of points would change nearly nothing about the distribution in that case. Conversely, having a weak prior (i.e. not much confidence) makes the data "speak" much earlier, at the cost of working with inflated uncertainty.

Second, the fact that we're still working with the logistic function (and its two degrees of freedom) means that we still see information gathered at one price propagating a long way towards other prices. The entire curve distribution still shifts when we observe behaviour at extreme prices, especially when that behaviour doesn't conform to the current uncertainty distribution.

Conclusion

We've seen that we can naturally incorporate uncertainty into our conversion modelling. Rather than working with a single conversion curve, we can work with a distribution of these curves. We've begged an important question here: how do we make decisions under this uncertainty?

OK, we're no longer making price decisions based on a single, over-confident model. We're now hedging our bets a little, but it's not yet clear how we make best use of these distributions. In future posts, we're going to move away from the detail of conversion modelling towards a fuller understanding of how we use a conversion model to set a price.
