Uncertainty and conversion modelling

Up to now, we've been trying to model how the underlying conversion rate depends on the price. Or, expressed another way, model what proportion of the population is willing to pay at least this price. And we've been going about it by trying to assert that we know the conversion rate at a given price; the proportion of the population willing to pay at least that price.

But all we have to go on are very few, sparse, observations of looks, some of which may be converted. Let's say we see five looks at a given price, but none of those booked. To what extent can we meaningfully infer the population-level conversion rate at this price? It may well be 0%: perhaps nobody at all was ever going to purchase at that price. But the conversion rate could easily be 5%, where we simply didn't see any of those customers willing to the pay that price within those 5 looks. Indeed, the conversion rate might be 50%, but our five "coin tosses" produced an unlucky five "tails" (no-buys) in a row.

Realistically, all we should say about this evidence of zero bookings from five looks is that it changes the relative likelihoods of the possible conversion rates. So we need a principled way of accounting for uncertainty in our modelling, which a Bayesian framework gives us very naturally.

In this post, we'll adapt the likelihood-driven approach to model fitting to the Bayesian approach of fitting a distribution of models to data.

Specifying model distributions

For simplicity, let's focus only on the logistic model for now. Recall that we can specify this conversion model by supplying the conversion rates at two reference prices. But now, let's not be so confident.

Instead, the conversion rates provided at each of the two reference prices are now understood as estimates. With those estimated rates, we will also provide measures of confidence in those estimates. In the interactive figure below, we have quantified this confidence to be interpreted as a certain number of "virtual" data points that support that estimate.

Having provided estimates of the uncertain conversion rates at the two specified prices, the plot above shows how the logistic conversion model induces the uncertain distribution of conversion rate, looking across the entire range of prices. For any given price, along the x axis, we can "slice" the curves above at that x value and read off our uncertainty in the underlying conversion rate at that price:

  • the lightest band covers the central 90% of the distribution,
  • the darker band covers the central 50% of the distribution,
  • and the central line represents the median of the distribution.
By changing the specified confidence in the two reference conversion rates, we can push the distribution bands narrower or wider.

Fitting a model distribution to data

As we previously saw from fitting a model to data, we can stream data on looks and conversions, and update our beliefs as we go. Up to now, we have been updating the best fit as data comes in, but here we will update the distribution sequentially as new data arrives.

As before, we can generate synthetic data by choosing a price and recording a look as either a conversion or rejection at that price. Observations

The first useful observation is the interaction between the strength of the prior and the new data. If we set a prior which is very confident in the two estimated conversion rates, say around a virtual sample size of 50, then we need a lot of data to override that. Adding only a handful of points would change nearly nothing about the distribution in that case. Conversely, having a weak prior (i.e. not much confidence) makes the data "speak" much earlier, at the cost of working with inflated uncertainty.

Second, the fact that we're still working with the logistic function (and its two degrees of freedom) means that we still see information gathered at one price propagating a long way, signficantly influencing the conversion rate distribution at other prices. The entire distribution still shifts when we observe behaviour at extreme prices, especially when that behaviour doesn't conform to the current uncertainty distribution.

Conclusion

We've seen that we can naturally incorporate uncertainty into our conversion modelling. Rather than working with a single conversion curve, we can work with a distribution of these curves. This was because we don't want to make price decisions based on a single, over-confident model. But we've begged an important question here: how do we make decisions under this uncertainty? To answer this question, in the next sequence of posts, we're going to move away from the detail of conversion modelling towards getting a fuller understanding of how we use a conversion model to set a price.
