Uncertainty and conversion modelling

Up to now, we've been trying answer the question, "what is the conversion curve?" That is, how does the underlying conversion rate depend on the price? Or, expressed another way, what proportion of the population is willing to pay at least this price? And we've been going about it by trying to find the conversion rate at a given price.

But all we have to go on are a few observations of looks, some of which turn into books. Say we see five looks at a given price and one of those booked. To what extent can we meaningfully conclude that the population-level conversion rate is 20%? Why not a lucky 5%, or an unlucky 50%? It's only five looks from your population, we could easily see a single book by random chance. How unlikely actually is an underlying 50% conversion rate?

That implies that there remains quite a range of possible conversion rates that underlie our observations. So, we need a principled way of accounting for uncertainty in our modelling, which a Bayesian framework gives us very naturally.

Specifying model distributions

Let's focus on the logistic model for now. We've previously talked about how to specify such a model based on its local behaviour at a single reference price. Suppose we supply those specifications once more, but now we emphasise that we're supplying only an estimated conversion rate and elasticity at that price. Together with these specifications, we also supply measures of confidence in them. This could be done two ways, both of which are shown in the example below:

  • A traditional standard deviation around the estimate, or
  • A "confidence", which can be interpreted as a certain number of 'virtual' data points supporting that estimate.

The plot above now represents a distribution of uncertain conversion rates according to our specification. Reading upwards at each price we can represent our uncertainty in the underlying conversion rate at that price:

  • the lightest band covers the central 90% of the distribution,
  • the darker band covers the central 50% of the distribution,
  • and the central line represents the median of the distribution.
Specifying a greater confidence in the reference conversion rate or a lower standard deviation in the reference elasticity, each push the distribution bands narrower.

Fitting a model distribution

As data streams in, we update a model distribution using a particle filter. This reweights curves in an sample as being more or less likely, given the data. When the weighting becomes so imbalanced that enough curves essentially "vanish", we rejuvenate the sample by resampling and exploring around the remaining best fits.
