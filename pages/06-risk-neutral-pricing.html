Maximising Expected Revenue

Recall our first introduction to conversion modelling. The idea was introduced that these models quantify what proportion of the population are willing to pay at least any given price. From the perspective of the seller observing an arrival process of a sequence of individuals in the population, the value of the conversion model at any given price is interpretable the probability that each arrival would pay that price.

In this post, we'll revisit the idea of using conversion models to set an optimal price. As usual, we'll consider only pricing a single product, without any constraints on capacity or time to sell. The price will be set to apply for one time unit, which might be an hour, day or week. At this stage, we're aiming to choose a price in order to maximise how much profit we expect to make in that time interval.

For the moment, let's step back from our exploration of uncertain conversion models and assume for now that we know exactly what our conversion model is. How do we price according to this known conversion model?

Modelling Profit with Arrival Processes

Before trying to price to optimise the expected profit accrued over our time unit of interest, we will need to think in more detail about how to use our modelling to relate price and profit.

In the introduction above we mentioned the idea of an arrival process, by which members of the population "look" at our price and make a buy/no-buy decision on whether to "book". In our explorations of fitting and uncertainty, this arrival process was the click of a button. In reality, arrivals are real potential customers arriving at random over our time unit.

Deterministic Arrivals

For the time unit we're pricing, let's drop the "at random" element for now, and make the (unrealistic) assumption that we know exactly how many looks we will get, which we denote by $n$.

Each of these arrivals decides whether to buy based on their willingness to pay, relative to the price, $u$. From our perspective, each potential customer decides independently at random, with probability $\phi(u)$, where $\phi$ is the known (for now) conversion model we've previously spent so long thinking about. If the customer decides not to buy, the net profit is zero. If the customer buys, that conversion generates $u - c$ net profit, for a known cost $c$. So, if $K$ represents the random number of conversions, our random profit is $P = (u-c)K$.

According to the process we described above, the random number of conversions is distributed like a binomial random variable, $$K \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, \phi(u)),$$ with expectation $n\phi(u)$. This means that the expected profit accrued in our time interval is given by $$\mathbf E(P) = (u-c) \mathbf E(K) = n(u-c)\phi(u).$$ Although the number of arrivals, $n$, appears in this expression, it is only as a linear scaling. The value of $u$ that optimises $\mathbf E(P)$ is independent of $n$.

To reiterate: for a deterministic arrival process, when maximising expected profit, the optimal price is independent of the number of arrivals. It only depends on the conversion model.

Random Arrivals

If a price is fixed for a time unit, then the conclusion arrived at above is moot. It's not realistic to know how many looks arrive in that time unit. What if our number of arrivals is a random variable, which we can denote by $N$?

The argument above got us to the conditional expectation $$\mathbf E(K \mid N=n) = n\phi(u).$$ By the law of total expectation, it follows that $$\mathbf E(K) = \mu \phi(u),$$ where $\mu = \mathbf E(N)$ is the average number of looks expected in the time unit. Since $P = (u-c)K$ we can write the expected profit in the time unit as $$\mathbf E(P) = \mu (u-c) \phi(u).$$ As with $n$ in the deterministic case, $\mu$ scales the expected profit linearly, but does not change the optimal price.

So we arrive at a stronger conclusion: for a random arrival process, when maximising expected profit, the optimal price is independent of the arrival process. It only depends on the conversion model.

Maximising Expected Profit

The argument above means that we can recommend a price $u^\star$ to maximise expected profit in a single time unit as $$u^\star = \arg \max_u \mu (u-c)\phi(u),$$ for an arbitrary $\mu$ modelling the expected number of looks in that time unit.

Beyond Risk-Neutral Pricing

Let's examine the curve representing expected profit at each price. It's easy to focus on the peak, but it's worth a thought experiment. Suppose you could only choose between two prices, one lower than the optimum and one higher, such that the expected profit was equal at both prices. In principle, by pricing according to this curve (that is, to maximise expected profit only), we're effectively saying that we're indifferent to the choice between these prices. This way of viewing our decision is known as risk-neutral.

In reality, is this actually the case? It's hard to view the two prices as equally "good" when the higher price produces a much more volatile outcome; sometimes higher profits with successful conversions, sometimes lower profits due to missed conversions. Yes, the two prices produce the same profit on average, but we can only play the game once: we are not going to see an average in reality. Perhaps we would systematically prefer to avoid gambling and take the more reliable outcome? What is the value we put on reliability?

In the next post, we will expand on this thought experiment and consider how risk aversion will affect our definition of optimality, influencing not only the price we choose but also significantly changing the influence of the looks arrival process on our decision-making.
