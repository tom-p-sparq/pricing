Maximising Expected Revenue

Recall our first introduction to conversion modelling. The idea was introduced that these models tell us what proportion of the population are willing to pay at least a given price. From the perspective of an arrival process of a sequence of individuals in the population, the conversion probability at a price is the probability that each arrival would pay that price.

In this post, we'll revisit the idea of using conversion models to set an optimal price. As usual, we'll consider only pricing a single product, without any constraints on capacity or time to sell. We assume that we set a price to apply for one time unit, which might be a day or week. At this stage, we're aiming to choose a price in order to maximise how much profit we expect to make in that time interval.

For the moment, let's step back from our exploration of uncertain conversion models and assume for now that we know exactly what our conversion model is. How do we price, given this model?

Modelling Profit with Arrival Processes

Before trying to price to optimise the profit accrued over our time unit of interest, we will need to think in more detail about how to use our modelling to relate price and profit.

In the introduction above we mentioned the idea of an arrival process, by which members of the population "look" at our price and make a buy/no-buy decision on whether to "book". In our explorations of fitting and uncertainty, this arrival process was the click of a button. In reality, arrivals are real potential customers arriving at random over our time unit.

Deterministic Arrivals

For the time unit we're pricing, let's drop the "at random" element for now, and make the (unrealistic) assumption that we know exactly how many looks we will get, which we denote by $n$.

Each of these arrivals decides whether to buy based on their willingness to pay, relative to the price, $u$. From our perspective, each potential customer decides independently at random, with probability $\phi(u)$, where $\phi$ is the known (for now) conversion model we've previously spent so long thinking about. If the customer decides not to buy, the net profit is zero. If the customer buys, that conversion generates $u - c$ net profit, for a known cost $c$. So, if $K$ represents the random number of conversions, our random profit is $P = (u-c)K$.

According to the process we described above, the random number of conversions is distributed like a binomial random variable, $$K \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, \phi(u)),$$ with expectation $n\phi(u)$. This means that the expected profit accrued in our time interval is given by $$\mathbf E(P) = (u-c) \mathbf E(K) = n(u-c)\phi(u).$$ The number of arrivals, $n$, does appear in this expression but only as a linear scaling. The value of $u$ that optimises $\mathbf E(P)$ is independent of $n$.

To reiterate: when maximising expected profit under a deterministic arrival process, the optimal price is independent of the number of arrivals. It only depends on the conversion model.

Random Arrivals

Of course, if pricing based on a time interval, then the conclusion arrived at above is moot: our assumption of a known, deterministic number of looks in our time unit is not very reasonable. What if our number of arrivals is a random variable, which we can denote by $N$?

The law of total expectation is $\mathbf E(K) = \mathbf E(\mathbf E(K \mid N))$. The inner conditional expectation is the random variable $N\phi(u)$, following the example above. The outer expectation is taken across the distribution of unknown $N$. So we can substitute this expression for $\mathbf E(K) = \phi(u)\mathbf E(N)$ to write $$\mathbf E(P) = (u-c)\mathbf E(K) = \mu (u-c)\phi(u),$$ where we've replaced the known number of looks, $n$, with the expectation of the unknown number of looks, $\mu = \mathbf E(N)$. Here, $\mu$ scales the expected profit linearly, but does not change the optimal price.

So we arrive at a stronger conclusion: when maximising expected profit under any price-independent arrival process, the optimal price is, conversely, independent of the arrival process. It only depends on the conversion model.

Maximising Expected Profit

The argument above means that we can recommend a price $u^\star$ to maximise expected profit in a single time unit as $$u^\star = \arg \max_u \mu (u-c)\phi(u),$$ with an arbitrary scale $\mu$ representing the average number of looks in that time unit.

Beyond Risk-Neutral Pricing

Let's examine the curve representing expected profit at each price. It's easy to get trapped looking for the peak, but it's worth a thought experiment here. Suppose you could only choose between two prices, one lower than the optimum and one higher, such that the expected profit was equal at both prices. In principle, by pricing according to this curve (that is, to maximise expected profit), we're effectively saying that there's no way to choose between two such prices. This way of viewing our decision is known as risk-neutral.

In reality, is this actually the case? Would we not prefer the increased likelihood of receiving a smaller amount at the lower price, given that, on average, the two prices will have the same result? In the next post, we will expand on this thought experiment and consider how risk aversion will affect our definition of "optimal", influencing not only the price we choose but also significantly changing the influence of the looks arrival process on our decision-making.
