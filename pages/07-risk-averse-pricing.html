Accounting for Risk

In our previous post, we looked at how to choose an optimal price for a given time unit. There, the optimality of a price was defined by maximising the expected profit achieved over that time unit.

That understanding of optimal is fairly typical of many revenue management applications. In this post, we'll revisit that definition, and think more carefully about whether optimising average behaviour is adequate.

Aleatoric Uncertainty and Profit Volatility

As in the previous post, we will continue, for now, to assume that we know our conversion model: there is no uncertainty in what $\phi(u)$ is. However, the variable $P$, representing the profit made in our single time unit, is still uncertain.

There are two main sources of uncertainty, known as aleatoric uncertainty. First, a random number of looks, $N$, arrives in our time unit. Second, the conversions are "at random", in the sense that each of the $N$ looks is a random draw from the population. This means that the number of conversions, $K$, is a random variable, and therefore that profit, $P = (u-c)K$, is random.

In the figure below we take a known conversion rate model, and assume we know exactly how many looks arrive in our time unit. We find the two prices that achieve some percentage of the theoretical maximum expected profit: the lower prioritises conversions, while the higher prioritises profit per conversion, but both will have the same expected total profit. We plot the actual distribution of the possible values of $P$ under each of those two prices.

The higher price clearly demonstrates a greater volatility in outcome. At the higher price, we may receive much more or much less in any one time unit. The lower price is significantly more predictable.

The argument of this post is that a firm should put a value on predictability. In the case above, if the distribution of outcomes of the lower price seem preferable, then the risk-neutral objective function cannot be the objective function to use for pricing. It treats the two distributions shown above as equally preferred, and that is not the case. So, to define the objective function that should be used instead, we need to quantify how much more preferable the profit distribution at the lower price is.

This doesn't just have consequences for comparing two prices with equal expected profit: by placing monetary value on the reliability of profit, it follows that we would prefer a slightly lower expected profit if it arrived with lower variance. So, rather than a straight maximisation of expected profit, we have a tradeoff to optimise.

Put succinctly: we are not typically risk-neutral. We are risk-averse, and should price accordingly.

Mean-Variance Optimisation

One classical approach to this trade-off is to penalise variance, by setting the objective function $$\mathbf E(P) - \rho \mathrm{Var}(P)$$ for the hyperparameter $\rho \geq 0$ quantifying our risk-aversion.

Recall that our model for profit is $P=(u-c)K$, for the uncertain number of conversions $K$ where $\left[ K \mid N=n \right] \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, \phi(u))$. Using the law of total expectation, we already found that $$\mathbf E(P) = \mu (u-c) \phi(u)$$ for $\mu = \mathbf E(N)$. Using the law of total variance we can similarly show that $$\mathrm{Var}(P) = (u-c)^2 \left[ \mu \phi(u) \left( 1-\phi(u) \right) + \sigma^2 \phi(u)^2 \right],$$ where $\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}(N)$ is the variance of the looks random variable.

Below, we can examine the effect of risk aversion on our objective function by playing with the slider setting the value of $\rho \geq 0$. We're still assuming deterministic $N=n$, meaning that $\mu=n$ and $\sigma^2 = 0$ in the formulae above.

Increased risk aversion leads to a reduction in the optimal price. By controlling this tradeoff between expectation and variance, we are effectively setting a reduction in expected profit that we are willing to incur in order for the profit we do get to arrive more predictibly, and with less volatility. Note also that, as in the risk-neutral case, the risk-averse optimal price is independent of the level of demand, as set by the number of looks.

So How Do We Price?

We've been holding some complexity back in this page. The arrival process underlying our exploration was simply a deterministic known $n$; we've plotted based on a fully known conversion model; we've played around with a risk-aversion parameter $\rho$ but not thought too hard about how to set it.

Over the next few posts, we'll go into much more detail about risk aversion and how aleatoric uncertainty (the results of random processes) and empirical uncertainty (unknown characteristics of the system) combine to influence our pricing decisions.
