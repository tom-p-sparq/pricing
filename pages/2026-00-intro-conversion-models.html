Conversion Modelling: an Introduction

Hello, and welcome to the first of a set of notes on conversion modelling! 🥳 We're going to introduce what conversion is, why and how we'd model it, and how we'd use a model of conversion to select a price.

Let's suppose that we have one or more products that we're selling, and we have potential customers arriving at our site, where they read all of our marketing copy and discover our price. At that point, they then make a decision as to whether they should buy one (or more) of our product(s).

As the vendor, we have a question that we are aiming to answer: "What price should we set for our products to achieve the best possible return?" This is an optimisation problem: a search for the "sweet spot" that balances what customers are willing to pay with our own revenue goals.

One vital ingredient for solving this problem is to have an understanding of the effect of price on whether a customer decides to purchase. We're going to explore how mathematical models can quantify the likelihood that a potential customer becomes a paying customer.

For now, let's focus on the simplest case: we have a large inventory of a single product line that we sell. And, if they buy, customers will only buy one of our products at a time. What price should we set our product line at?

Conversion and willingness to pay

We will be thinking about our optimisation problem in terms of probabilities. When a customer arrives at our site, we don't know exactly what they're willing to pay for our product. We can think of them as a random draw from a population of potential customers. Some of those potential customers are willing to pay a lot; some, less.

Let's use $V$ to represent the maximum price a randomly chosen customer is willing to pay for our product. Then, that customer will purchase at a price $p$ if $V \geq p$. In other words, a sale happens if our price is less than or equal to their willingness to pay.

So we now have the concept of a conversion probability. If we model how willingness to pay is distributed across the population of potential customers, then we are, in effect, modeling the probability that the next customer who arrives will make a purchase.

Conversion Models

Below is a simple example of a typical conversion model. Along the $x$ axis is the price, and along the $y$ axis is the conversion probability at that price. That is, the $y$ axis is the fraction of the population whose willingness to pay, $V$, is at least that price.

In this example, we're modelling the population with a median willingness to pay of \$150, meaning that if we set the price at \$150, we'd expect about half of our potential customers to buy. So, when a new customer arrives on the site, to the best of our knowledge, there is a 50% chance they will purchase at that price.

This plot demonstrates some typical characteristics of a conversion model based on the idea of willingness to pay.

  • Monotonically decreasing: as prices increase, we filter out more of the population, until (almost) nobody is willing to pay that much.
  • Conversely, at very low prices, nearly everyone who wants the product will buy.
  • Sigmoid ("S") shape: the curve is steepest near the middle, suggesting that many customers have a similar willingness to pay around a central point.
Like any model, we can think of various ways in which this approach is a rough approximation of reality. Two that readily spring to mind are (A) the psychological effects of round numbers and "sticky prices", and (B) the way in which price signals quality, such that we can't see very high conversion at the lowest prices. For now, we will ignore these effects, but it would be interesting to think about where they become important.

Conversion and incremental revenue

Now that we have a conversion model, how does it help us?

When our potential customer arrives and sees a price $p$, we now have a model for the probability that they will make a purchase. This gives us a handle on the expected financial outcome of that visit. Specifically, we can model the incremental revenue (that is, revenue less marginal cost of sale) that this customer might provide.

Let's write $\phi(p)$ for the conversion probability at a given price $p$: this is what was plotted in the figure above, as $p$ varied. This means that with probability $\phi(p)$ we make an incremental revenue of $p - c$, where $c$ is the marginal cost of sale. Otherwise, we make no incremental revenue.

Therefore the expected incremental revenue at price $p$ is given by the function $$R(p) = (p - c) \cdot \phi(p).$$ In the figure below, using the conversion model $\phi$ we plotted above, we show $R(p)$ as a function of $p$.

As we vary the incremental cost, we can see how the expected incremental revenue on the $y$ axis varies with price along the $x$ axis.

  • When the price is below cost, we would expect to make a loss.
  • As the price increases above cost, we begin to make a profit.
  • At some critical price level, for any further increase in price, the incremental revenue we would expect to achieve begins to decrease. This is because we are being too greedy: the amount of additional margin we would make is offset by the loss in conversions we would experience.
That critical price is where the expected incremental revenue is maximised: the peak in the figure above.

Our optimisation problem, as initially described, was deliberately vague. We talked about finding a price to "get the best possible return". If we are explicit that "best return" is defined as "the largest expected incremental revenue per visitor," then the peak in our figure is the solution to our optimisation problem. In other words, we can read off the optimal price, according to this conversion model, directly from this figure.

And so, we have used a conversion model to make a pricing decision.

And we can already answer some nice questions. Let's say our incremental cost of sale increases from \$50 to \$80. Some of that increase should be absorbed, and some passed to the customer. What does our model say is the optimal split to ensure the best possible outcome in the face of these increased costs? It's as simple as sliding our input cost and reading off the change in optimal price.

Where do we go from here?

We've taken our first step into the world of conversion modelling, seeing how a simple model can help us reason about one of the most fundamental questions in business: "What price should we set?"

But this is just the beginning of the journey. Now that we have the foundational concepts of willingness-to-pay and expected revenue, we can explore the richer, more detailed landscape of pricing strategy. For now, we will park the broader question of price optimisation and dive further into the science of modelling conversion itself.

Here is the path we'll take in upcoming posts:

  • A "Zoo" of Parametric Models: We'll meet a whole family of conversion models, each with its own personality and assumptions about customer behavior.
  • Bringing Models to Life with Data: We'll learn how to take these abstract models and fit them to actual sales data, turning theory into a practical tool.
  • From Data to Decisions: Once we have a fitted model, what can we do with it? We'll explore how to use our models to predict, forecast, and even simulate entire market scenarios.

And beyond that, the horizon is wide open. We'll be equipped to tackle even more complex and realistic questions: How do we price multiple products that might compete with or complement each other? How do we account for a market that changes over time? What if we want to tailor prices for different customer segments? These are the challenges where modelling truly shines.
