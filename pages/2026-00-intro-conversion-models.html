Conversion Modelling: an Introduction

Hello, and welcome to the first of a set of notes on conversion modelling! 🥳 We're going to introduce what conversion is, why and how we'd model it, and how we'd use a model of conversion to select a price.

Let's suppose that we have one or more products that we're selling, and we have potential customers arriving at our site, where they read all of our marketing copy and discover our price. At that point, they then make a decision as to whether they should buy one (or more) of our product(s).

As the vendor, we have a question that we are aiming to answer: "What price should we be setting for our products to get the best possible return?" This is an optimisation problem.

One vital ingredient for solving this problem is to have an understanding of the effect of price on whether a customer decides to purchase. We're going to talk about how we can use mathematical models to quantify the likelihood that a potential customer becomes an actual customer.

For now, let's focus on the simplest case: we have a very large inventory of one product line that we sell. And, if they buy, customers will only buy one of our products at a time. What price should we set our product line at?

Conversion and willingness to pay

We'll be thinking about our optimisation problem in terms of probabilities. When a customer arrives at our site, let's say (for now) we don't yet know who they are, nor what they're willing to pay for our product. So we can think of them as being a random draw from a population of potential customers. Some of those potential customers are willing to pay a lot; some, less.

Let's write $V$ as the random variable corresponding to the willingness to pay of a randomly selected customer. Then, that customer will purchase at a price $p$ if $V \geq p$. That is, if the customer is willing to pay at least that price.

So we now have the concept of a conversion probability. If we model how willingness to pay is distributed across the population of potential customers, then we are equivalently modelling the probability that the randomly selected customer (that is, the arrival at our site) will make a purchase.

Conversion Models

Below is a simple example of a typical conversion model. Along the $x$ axis is the price, and along the $y$ axis is the conversion probability. That is, the $y$ axis is the fraction of the population whose willingness to pay, $V$, is at least that price.

In this example, we're modelling the population with a median willingness to pay of \$150, such that 50% of potential customers are not willing to pay that much, while 50% will pay at least that amount. So, when a customer arrives on the site, to our knowledge there is a probability of 0.5 that they will purchase at that price.

This plot demonstrates some typical characteristics of a conversion model based on the idea of willingness to pay.

  • Monotonically decreasing: as prices increase, we filter out more of the population, until (almost) nobody is willing to pay that much.
  • Conversely, at low, low prices, everyone will buy!
  • Sigmoid shape: most of the population has a similar willingness to pay, at the prices where the rate of decrease in conversion is greatest.
Like any model, we can think of various ways in which this approach is a rough approximation of reality. Two that readily spring to mind are (A) the psychological effects of round numbers and "sticky prices", and (B) the way in which price signals quality, such that we can't see very high conversion at the lowest prices. For now, we will ignore these effects, but it would be interesting to think about where they become important.

Conversion and incremental revenue

Now we have a conversion model, how does that actually help us?

When our potential customer arrives and sees a price $p$, we now have an idea of the probability that the potential customer makes a purchase at that price. This means that we have a handle on the distribution of the random variable corresponding to the amount of incremental revenue (that is, revenue less marginal cost of sale) that the customer may provide.

Let's write $\phi(p)$ for the conversion probability at price $p$: this is what was plotted in the figure above, as $p$ varied. This means that with probability $\phi(p)$ we make an incremental revenue of $p - c$, where $c$ is the marginal cost of sale. Otherwise, we make no incremental revenue.

Therefore the expected incremental revenue at price $p$ is given by the function $$R(p) = (p-c) \cdot \phi(p).$$ In the figure below, using the conversion model $\phi$ we plotted above, we show $R(p)$ as a function of $p$.

As we vary the incremental cost, we can see how the expected incremental revenue on the $y$ axis varies with price along the $x$ axis.

  • When the price is below cost, we would expect to make a loss.
  • As the price increases above cost, we begin to make a profit.
  • At some critical price level, for any further increase in price, the incremental revenue we would expect to achieve begins to decrease. This is because we are being too greedy: the amount of additional margin we would make is offset by the loss in conversions we would experience.
That critical price is where the expected incremental revenue is maximised: the peak in the figure above.

Now, our optimisation problem that we initially described was (deliberately) vaguely worded. We talked about finding a price to "get the best possible return". If we're explicit that we're defining the best possible return to be "the largest expected incremental revenue per potential customer", then the peak in our figure is the solution to our optimisation problem. In other words, we can read off the optimal price, according to this conversion model, directly from this figure.

And so we will have used a conversion model to make a pricing decision!

And we can already answer some nice questions. Let's say our incremental cost of sale increases from \$50 to \$80. Some of that increase should be absorbed, and some passed to the customer. What does our model say is the optimal split to ensure the best possible outcome in the face of these increased costs? It's as simple as sliding our input cost and reading off the change in optimal price.

Where do we go from here?

This was the first of a series of notes on pricing and conversion modelling. We've looked at an example of a conversion model and how it can be used to help decide price, under a very specific understanding of optimal pricing. For now, we will park the question of how to price and deep-dive into modelling conversion probabilities. The current plan is to look at:

  • Parametric conversion models: a zoo of conversion models

There are all sorts of directions to go in though, even just on conversion modelling and ignoring price optimisation. What about multiple product lines, and multiple purchases? What about time-varying markets and dynamics? What about non-parametric models? What about market segmentation?
