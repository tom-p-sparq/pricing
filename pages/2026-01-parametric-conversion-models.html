A Conversion Modelling Zoo

In our previous post we introduced the idea of conversion modelling: the specification of a curve mapping prices to the probability that a customer would convert at that price. We talked about how this would form the basis of predicting incremental revenue, and being able to simulate markets. Typically, these curves would be decreasing with increasing price, starting from a near-1 conversion probability at low prices and decreasing to zero at high prices.

What we didn't talk about were the details of how we would go about drawing those curves.

In this post, we'll consider some possible parametric forms that a conversion model might take. By 'parametric form', we mean families of curves that can be defined by just a couple of economically meaningful values. We'll show these families first, before exploring how changing parameters can move those shapes around. The (non-exhaustive) list of conversion probability families we will consider is:

  • Linear
  • Logistic
  • Log-logistic
  • Weibull
  • Constant elasticity

Linear model

Perhaps the simplest model, conversion is modelled as depending on price such that a change in price by some delta maps to a change in conversion probability proportional to that delta. For positive parameters $a$ and $b$, we can specify the linear form, $$\tilde \phi(p) = a - bp$$ as our first model.

The plot below shows this linear form for a particular choice of $a$ and $b$.1

What are the characteristics of this linear model? For every \$15 price increase (or decrease), the conversion probability is decreased (or increased) by 10 percentage points. According to this model, this means that beyond \$225 the conversion probability is modelled as zero; similarly, for prices less than \$75, every potential customer will purchase.

Logistic model

Data scientists may recognise our conversion modelling goals as fitting neatly into the logistic regression framework. We have 0/1 observations of a purchase, and a feature (price). So, it makes sense to model conversion by the sigmoid of a linear form, $$\phi(p) = \sigma(a - bp),$$ where $\sigma(z) = \exp(z) / (1 + \exp(z))$. Equivalently, we can write this as a model of the logit of conversion probability of a linear form such that $$\log\left(\frac{\phi(p)}{1 - \phi(p)}\right) = a-bp.$$ In either case, $b$ is assumed to be positive for conversion to decrease with respect to price.

Below, we plot conversion probability $\phi$ as a function of price $p$ according to the logistic model for a particular choice of $a$ and $b$:

The modelled behaviour is less extreme than the linear model. There is a longer "tail" of positive conversion probability at higher prices, and by definition the model never predicts conversion probabilities of exactly zero or one. The gradient changes smoothly, so that the response of the model to price shifts is different at lower and higher prices.

Log-logistic model

When considering the response of markets to price shifts, relative values are often more important than absolute values. In the two models above, we use a linear form inside either a "clamp" or sigmoid operator, in the sense of being linear in price. If we want to model behaviour based on relative prices, we can use a quasi-linear form of $a - b\log(p)$ instead. This induces the log-logistic conversion model, $$\log\left(\frac{\phi(p)}{1 - \phi(p)}\right) = a - b\log(p).$$ With some algebraic manipulation, this is equivalent to the model $$\phi(p) = \frac{1}{1 + \left( p / p_0 \right)^b},$$ where the new parameter $p_0$ can be written in terms of $a$ and $b$.

Below, we plot the conversion probability $\phi$ as a function of $p$ according to the log-logistic model for a particular choice of $a$ and $b$:

The key differences to note between the logistic and log-logistic models are at the tails. For lower prices, the log-logistic model is much faster to approach 1; for higher prices, the log-logistic model is much slower to approach 0. The heavier tails of this model mean that it is well suited for markets where there are a small number of customers willing to pay particularly high prices versus the majority.

Weibull model

The fatter tails at high prices of the log-logistic model, or even the logistic model, can sometimes feel unrealistic in markets where there is a lot of competition and differentiation is difficult. We may easily have a "cliff-edge" behaviour at some market baseline, where prices above that baseline lead to low conversion rates as customers decide to purchase with a competitor.

The Weibull model is well-suited to this. Instead of modelling the logit of conversion, we model the complementary log-log of conversion as a function of log-price: $$-\log(-\log(\phi(p))) = a - b\log(p).$$ With some algebraic manipulation, this is equivalent to the survival function of a Weibull distribution, $$\phi(p) = \exp\left(-\left(\frac{p}{p_0}\right)^b\right),$$ where, as with the log-logistic model, $p_0$ is a function of $a$ and $b$. The parameter $b$ is known as the shape parameter, and it controls how sharp the drop-off in conversion is. For larger $b$, we would see a much more pronounced "cliff-edge".

Constant elasticity model

Finally, we consider a slightly different model to all of the others above. This is not sigmoidal, and does not tend to 1 at low prices. This model is particularly simple as it is linear in log-log space: $$\log(\phi(p)) = a - b \log(p).$$ When manipulating this to write $\phi$ as a function of $p$, we have the constant elasticity model of the form $$\phi(p) = c p^{-\eta},$$ where $\eta > 0$ is known as the elasticity of demand. The constants $c$ and $\eta$ can be written in terms of $a$ and $b$ from the linear representation above.

This model is often used in economics for its simplicity and clear interpretation. However, it has some drawbacks for general-purpose conversion modeling. It predicts that conversion probability goes to infinity as price approaches zero, and it never reaches zero for any finite price.
