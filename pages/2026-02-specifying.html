Specifying Conversion Model Parameters

So far, we've introduced conversion modelling, and explored a few example parametric forms that conversion models might take. In this post we'll go a little deeper into how we might specify those models, in the sense of selecting the parameters.

Local definition

In our conversion modelling zoo we compared all of our species against one another in a single plot. For a fair comparison we gave them all the same behaviour at one specific reference price.

The key observation here is that once a conversion model form (e.g. logistic) has been chosen, it's not enough to specify the conversion rate at a particular reference price. There are many logistic conversion curves that give that conversion probability at that price.

If, however, we also specify the local behaviour of the conversion curve around that price, then we uniquely specify the shape of the curve across the entire range of prices. Parametric modellers often refer to the "degrees of freedom" in their model. In our case, all of the models we specified in our zoo have two degrees of freedom: specifying a location (via a price/conversion pair) and a shape (via elasticity at that point) is sufficient to determine the entire curve.

On Elasticity

We have mentioned the word "elasticity" in this post and previously. It is a description of the conversion probability curve in terms of how fast conversion is changing with price. We could use the more classical and accessible derivative, $\mathrm d\phi(p) / \mathrm dp$, for this description. One problem with this quantity is that it is dimensional: it tells us the percentage point change in conversion rate per unit currency. However, a £1 price increase is a very different concept at $p=1$ versus $p=100$. Similarly, a 5% percentage point change in conversion rate is a very different concept at $\phi=0.05$ versus $\phi=0.5$.

In response to this dimensionality in the derivative, using elasticity to quantify the sensitivity of conversion probability to price becomes useful. Mathematically, it is defined as $$\epsilon(p) = \frac{\mathrm d\log(\phi(p))}{\mathrm d\log(p)} = \frac{p}{\phi(p)} \frac{\mathrm d\phi(p)}{\mathrm dp},$$ whereby the derivative is normalised by $p/\phi$. This normalisation means that elasticity is non-dimensional: it describes the percentage change in conversion probability that our model predicts from a percentage change in price. For example, say conversion at price $p=100$ was $\phi(100) = 0.5$. An elasticity of $\epsilon(100) = -2$ would then correspond to a model predicting conversion at price $p=101$ of $\phi(101) \approx (1 - 0.02) \times \phi(100) = 0.49$.

The key consequence of normalisation is that a given value of elasticity has the same meaning across the entire range of prices and conversion probabilities. In other words, elasticity is an economically meaningful value, and we don't need to know its contextual price and corresponding conversion probability to interpret it.

An alternative specification: interpolation

We saw above that having an estimate of conversion probability at one reference price point is not sufficient to determine the conversion probability curve. This is because the curves we're dealing with have two degrees of freedom: one point only specifies one of those degrees of freedom.

Above, we used elasticity to nail down the other degree of freedom. Alternatively, we can uniquely determine a conversion curve by specifying another point it has to travel through. If we have estimates of the conversion rate at two different prices, then that's enough to specify the global behaviour of each conversion probability curve across the entire price range.

From playing around with the two methods for specifying the models in our zoo, it becomes clear that the differences between models can only be made very small in small regions of the price range. As we extrapolate further from our reference price(s), and as our interpolants move far apart in price space, our models demonstrate very significant differences in behaviour.
